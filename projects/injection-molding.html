Desktop PLA Recycling Injection Molding Machine

Attribution: This machine is not an original design. It is a student build that closely follows the open DIY Buster Beagle 3D MK3 pneumatic injection molding machine, and it uses Buster Beagle 3D's heating chamber, funnel, band heaters, mold sprue and mold template. Our team adapted, built, analyzed and tested that approach for a university lab, and added safety features. Full credit for the underlying design goes to Buster Beagle 3D.

A team build for Stony Brook's Senior Design Lab and its engineering makerspace that turns PLA 3D-printing waste into molded parts. We adapted the Buster Beagle 3D MK3 pneumatic design and added safety features, temperature control and engineering analysis for a university lab.

The problem

3D printing produces a steady stream of waste: support material, failed prints and abandoned prototypes, mostly PLA, which isn't easily recycled. We estimated that the Senior Design Lab and the makerspace generate about 15 kg of it per year. The goal was a user-friendly benchtop machine that turns shredded and pelletized PLA waste back into useful molded parts, and that lets students see injection molding up close.

What we built

  • Vertical, pneumatic injection machine on a 2020 aluminum extrusion frame, about 24 in tall, running on a standard 120 V benchtop.
  • Heating: a stainless steel MK3 chamber with two band heaters, a PID controller and a K-type thermocouple, melting PLA pellets at about 220 °C.
  • Injection: a 4 in bore, 12 in stroke pneumatic cylinder driven through a solenoid valve from an external air compressor.
  • Mold retention: a 4 in low-profile drill press vise (2,000 lb clamping force) holding a 3D-printed resin mold, with an aluminum sprue protecting the mold from direct nozzle contact.
  • Spring-mounted chamber: the heating chamber rides on linear rods with compression springs, so a slightly misaligned mold or a ram hitting unmelted plastic doesn't shock the frame. Even 0.5 mm of mold misalignment can cause flashing.
  • Safety: a polycarbonate enclosure with a magnetic door interlock, an emergency stop that cuts power to the heaters and solenoid, an insulated chamber and high-limit temperature control. We designed against OSHA 29 CFR 1910.212 and ANSI B11.0.

My contribution

  • Created the first two concept designs: a vertical machine with a pneumatic piston and heated chamber, and a vertical machine with a band heater and hand crank. Both were later redesigned.
  • Assembled the final production CAD in Fusion 360, sourced and modeled its components, and produced the component drawings.
  • Calculated the loads on the pneumatic piston mounting plates and the required mold clamping force.
  • Wrote the report's introduction chapter and researched two of the patents in our patent review.

The heat-transfer, injection-pressure and hoop-stress analyses, the electrical schematics and the BOM were done by teammates.

Testing and results

  • Emergency stop: at full operating temperature (220 °C), pressing the e-stop immediately cut power to both heaters and the solenoid valve. The magnetic door interlock worked.
  • Heat-up: with 30 g of PLA pellets, the chamber thermocouple reached 220 °C in about 8 minutes, and the PLA was fully melted at about 10 minutes. We recommend a 15-minute total soak from a cold start.
  • First three mold runs failed: the molten PLA never entered the cavity. The cause was trapped air with no escape path, which created back pressure. A larger inlet gate and a vent hole at the base of the cavity fixed it, with no changes to the machine.
  • First successful part: a smooth, nearly fully filled, quarter-shaped part with minimal flash. About 15 g of the 30 g shot went into the mold. The final design needs about 51.7 psi of injection pressure.

Limitations

  • The mold was printed in standard clear resin, because the high-temperature resin order wasn't enough to run the printer. It deformed after the cycle, so it would last only a handful of shots.
  • We demonstrated a single-part proof of concept, not repeatable production.

What I learned

  • The full engineering process, from ideation to a working machine, including the calculations needed to make a design real.
  • Production-level CAD in Fusion 360 and SolidWorks.
  • How a PID controller actually behaves in a real machine, beyond the coursework theory.
  • Injection molding as a process, and how mold details like venting and gating decide whether a part fills.
