I-Corps Customer Discovery: Testing a Hydrogen Technology with 20 Industry Experts

A three-person team spent three weeks interviewing industry experts to find out whether anyone would actually want a device that both produces hydrogen and generates electricity. What we heard changed our target market, our business model and our pitch.

The challenge

The technology was a single device that can work both as a solid oxide electrolyzer, making hydrogen, and as a fuel cell, making electricity. On paper it could produce low-cost hydrogen and power. But nobody on the team knew who would pay for it, or why. The program's rule was to find out from real people instead of guessing.

What we did

We drafted a Business Model Canvas, wrote down our assumptions as testable hypotheses, and built an interview pipeline from scratch through research labs, professors' networks, company contacts, patent authors and LinkedIn. At one point about 30 requests were pending. The people we spoke with included:

  • National laboratory researchers, and university professors and researchers
  • Engineers and executives at hydrogen and electrolyzer companies
  • Renewable energy companies and a hydrogen transport company
  • A U.S. Army energy advisor, and policy and market experts

How our thinking changed

  1. What we believedCustomers want the most efficient electrolyzer, and high-temperature electrolysis is better because it's more efficient. We had a broad list of customers, from hydrogen producers to automotive.
  2. What we heardEfficiency numbers mean little on their own: customers compare against existing electrolyzers and want cost parity. A PEM electrolyzer company told us the industry is actively lowering operating temperatures, because heat degrades membranes faster. Storing and transporting hydrogen, not making it, was the real pain point. And not all waste heat is equal: the device needs a steady heat source.
  3. What we changedWe reframed the value around making power on-site (no transport, less storage), a longer lifespan and lower operating temperature, not raw efficiency. We narrowed the target market to sites with steady high-temperature waste heat, especially nuclear and micro-nuclear plants, and rewrote the canvas.

Other findings

  • Licensing is hard to protect. Competitors can tweak a composition just outside a patent's claims, and companies run their own evaluations, so many outside ideas are "considered but shelved".
  • Policy matters. Companies want to capture the full U.S. clean-hydrogen tax credit, so faster start-up and less degradation from cycling make a technology more attractive.
  • Who decides: we mapped who recommends, who buys and who can block a deal in each customer segment. For example, engineers can act as a recommender or a saboteur, while executives make the final call.

We also compared licensing against manufacturing the device, and a small-scale (0.1 MW) product against a larger (1 MW) one. At that point we judged the small-scale option, paired with small nuclear sites, the better fit. Finally, we wrote a $1,000 budget request for more discovery, covering a clean-energy summit for in-person interviews and a LinkedIn Premium account for outreach, plus a plan to test the technology with a lab partner before any prototyping.

Limits

This was customer discovery, not a built product. We didn't build or test hardware, and any performance figures in our pitch were the technology's claims, not our measurements.
